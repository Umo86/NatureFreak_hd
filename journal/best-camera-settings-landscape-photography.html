The best camera settings for landscape photography (with a cheat sheet)

There is no magic combination of numbers, but there is a sensible starting point for every kind of landscape. Here's what to set, why, and when to change it.

By the NATUREFreak teamUpdated 13 November 20267 min read

The short version

  • Default: aperture priority or manual, f/8–f/11, base ISO, tripod, RAW.
  • Focus a third of the way into the scene, or use hyperfocal distance, or focus stack for very close foregrounds.
  • Avoid f/16–f/22 unless you need a starburst or a longer exposure; diffraction softens the whole frame.
  • Set white balance to Daylight so sunrise and sunset colours aren't "corrected" away.
  • Protect the highlights. Expose so the sky isn't clipped, then lift shadows in editing.

Ask ten landscape photographers for their settings and you'll get ten slightly different answers, but they'll all land in roughly the same place. That's because landscapes ask for the same things nearly every time: a lot of the scene in focus, as much detail and as little noise as possible, and control over how movement looks.

This guide explains each setting in that light, then gives a cheat sheet for the situations you'll meet most. If you want the bigger picture on light and composition first, read our landscape photography tips.

The cheat sheet

"Base" means your camera's lowest native ISO, usually 100 or 64. "A/Av" is aperture priority. Use RAW throughout.

Aperture: f/8 to f/11 is the sweet spot

Aperture controls depth of field, how much of the scene from front to back appears sharp. Landscapes usually want a lot of it, which is why beginners are often told to "use f/22". That advice is out of date.

Every lens has a range where it's sharpest, usually two or three stops down from wide open. Close the aperture much further and diffraction sets in: light spreading as it passes through a small opening softens fine detail across the whole frame. On a full-frame camera, diffraction becomes visible somewhere around f/11 to f/16, earlier on high-resolution sensors and smaller formats. Cambridge in Colour explains the physics well.

So the best aperture for most landscape work is:

  • Full frame: f/8 to f/11
  • APS-C: f/5.6 to f/8
  • Micro Four Thirds: f/4 to f/5.6
  • Phones: fixed; depth of field is already huge

Use smaller apertures only for a reason: a starburst on the sun, extra depth of field when you can't focus stack, or a slightly longer shutter speed.

Focus: where to put it

Getting the focus right matters more than the aperture. Three methods, from simplest to most precise:

1. Focus a third of the way in. With a wide or standard lens at f/8–f/11 and no very close foreground, focusing on something about a third of the way into the scene gets nearly everything acceptably sharp.

2. Hyperfocal distance. For a given focal length and aperture, there's a focus distance at which everything from half that distance to infinity is acceptably sharp. At 16 mm and f/11 on full frame it's roughly 0.8 metres, so in theory everything from about 40 cm to the horizon is acceptably sharp. A hyperfocal calculator gives the numbers. Treat them as a minimum and focus slightly further.

3. Focus stacking. When your foreground is very close (a flower a few centimetres from the lens) no single aperture can hold it and the mountains sharp. Take several frames, focusing at different distances, and blend them in Photoshop, Affinity, Lightroom Classic's companion tools or dedicated software. Many cameras can do this automatically with "focus bracketing".

Use manual focus or autofocus then switch it off, magnify on the rear screen to check, and refocus if you change composition.

Shutter speed: decide what movement should look like

On a tripod, shutter speed is mostly a consequence of your aperture and ISO. It becomes a creative choice when something moves: water, clouds, grass, trees, people.

  • 1/500 s and faster: freezes spray and wave tips.
  • 1/15 – 1/4 s: water has texture and some flow; good for small cascades.
  • 0.5 – 2 s: classic silky waterfall.
  • 5 – 30 s: waves become mist; clouds smear.
  • 1 – 4 minutes: clouds become streaks; water is glass. Needs a strong ND filter in daylight.

When handheld, keep the shutter at least 1/focal length (1/50 s at 50 mm), and faster on high-resolution sensors. Image stabilisation buys several stops for static scenes.

ISO: stay at base whenever you can

Base ISO gives the most dynamic range and the cleanest files, which matters in landscapes, where you'll often lift shadows or pull back a bright sky. On a tripod there's rarely a reason to move from it.

Raise ISO when handheld, when wind is moving leaves and you need a faster shutter, or at night. Modern sensors are clean up to ISO 1600–3200, and denoising software handles more.

Mode: aperture priority or manual?

Aperture priority is quick: you set f/8 and ISO 100, the camera chooses the shutter speed. Use exposure compensation to brighten or darken. It's ideal when light changes fast.

Manual keeps exposure fixed, which you need for panoramas (so frames match), bracketing, long exposures with ND filters, and night photography.

Many landscape photographers work in aperture priority until the light gets tricky, then switch to manual.

Metering and exposure: protect the highlights

Digital sensors handle underexposure better than overexposure. Once a bright sky clips to pure white, the detail is gone; shadows can usually be lifted.

  • Turn on the histogram and highlight warnings ("blinkies").
  • Expose so the brightest important part of the scene sits just left of the right edge of the histogram.
  • When the range is too wide (sun in the frame, dark foreground), bracket: three to five frames at 1–2 stops apart, then blend.

Evaluative or matrix metering is fine as a start; spot metering is useful for measuring the sky directly at sunrise.

White balance: Daylight beats Auto at sunset

Auto white balance tries to neutralise colour casts, which is exactly what you don't want at golden hour. Set Daylight (around 5200–5600 K) so warm light stays warm and blue hour stays blue. Since you're shooting RAW you can change it later, but a sensible setting means your preview and histogram reflect the scene.

Other settings worth checking

  • RAW, not JPEG.
  • Image stabilisation off on a tripod, unless your camera detects tripods automatically.
  • Two-second timer or remote release to avoid shaking the camera when you press the button.
  • Electronic front curtain or silent shutter on long exposures to reduce vibration.
  • Long exposure noise reduction off for most work (it doubles your wait), on for very long exposures in warm weather if hot pixels appear.
  • Electronic level on in the viewfinder.

Settings for golden hour

Golden hour light is warm, low and changes quickly, which catches people out.

  1. White balance on Daylight.
  2. Aperture priority, f/8, base ISO, tripod.
  3. Exposure compensation −0.3 to −0.7 EV to keep the colour rich and highlights intact.
  4. If shooting into the sun, switch to manual and bracket.
  5. Re-check the histogram every few minutes; the light drops faster than you expect.

Putting it together: a routine

  1. Compose, level and lock the tripod.
  2. Set base ISO, f/8–f/11, Daylight white balance, RAW.
  3. Focus a third in, or on the foreground; check by magnifying.
  4. Take a test frame. Read the histogram, not the screen.
  5. Adjust shutter speed (or exposure compensation) to protect the highlights.
  6. Decide on movement: faster to freeze, slower to smooth.
  7. Bracket if the range is too wide; focus stack if the foreground is too close.

The settings are only half the work. The other half is being there when the light is worth photographing. When you are ready to stay out after dark, the Milky Way guide is the natural next step, and if you only have a phone with you, try long exposures on an iPhone.

Questions people ask

What is the best aperture for landscape photography?

f/8 to f/11 on full-frame cameras gives the best balance of depth of field and sharpness. On APS-C, f/5.6 to f/8 behaves similarly, and on Micro Four Thirds f/4 to f/5.6. Smaller apertures increase depth of field but soften detail through diffraction.

What ISO should I use for landscapes?

Your camera's base ISO, usually 100 (64 on some bodies), whenever you're on a tripod. Raise ISO only when handheld or when you need a faster shutter speed to freeze moving leaves, waves or people.

What shutter speed is best for landscape photography?

Whatever gives a correct exposure at your chosen aperture and ISO, unless there's movement. For smooth water try 0.5–2 seconds, for misty waves 5–30 seconds, and for freezing a crashing wave 1/250 s or faster.

Should I use manual or aperture priority for landscapes?

Either works. Aperture priority is quick and accurate in changing light. Manual is better for long exposures, bracketing and panoramas, where you need exposure to stay the same between frames.
