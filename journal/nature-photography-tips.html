Nature photography tips: 21 habits that actually improve your pictures

Most advice about nature photography is about gear. Almost none of what follows is. These are the habits that change pictures, sorted by when you need them.

By the NATUREFreak teamUpdated 13 November 20268 min read

The short version

  • Shoot in the first and last two hours of daylight; plan the rest of the day around scouting.
  • Pick one subject per frame and make everything else support it, or remove it.
  • Learn one place well. You will get more good pictures from fifty visits to a local wood than one trip to Iceland.
  • Use a shutter speed fast enough for the subject, then let ISO rise. Noise is fixable; blur is not.
  • Leave the subject as you found it. No picture is worth stressing an animal or trampling a habitat.

A lot of nature photography advice starts with what to buy. That is the least useful place to start. Two people with the same camera, standing in the same wood, will come home with very different pictures, and the difference is almost never the equipment. It is when they went, where they stood, what they left out and how patient they were.

The tips below are grouped in the order you meet the problems: before you go, while you are out, when you press the shutter and afterwards. Some are about technique. More of them are about habits, because habits are what you still have when the light changes and there is no time to think.

Before you leave the house

1. Plan for light, not for places

The same lake can be dull at noon and remarkable forty minutes after sunrise. Decide when you will shoot first, and then where. For most outdoor subjects the best light falls in the first and last two hours of the day, when the sun is low enough to throw long shadows and show texture. Midday is not wasted time: it is when you scout, walk the route and work out where you will stand when the light is good.

Free tools help. Any weather app with cloud cover by altitude will tell you whether a sunrise has a chance. Apps such as PhotoPills or The Photographer's Ephemeris show where the sun will rise and set against the landscape on any date, which is how photographers know a sun will line up with a valley months in advance.

2. Learn one place properly

The most reliable way to improve is unglamorous: choose somewhere within half an hour of home and keep going back. A local wood, a reservoir, a stretch of coast. After a dozen visits you will know where the deer cross, which corner catches sunrise in winter and when the bluebells peak. Pictures that look lucky are usually the result of that kind of knowledge.

Keep a short note on your phone for each visit: date, weather, what was happening, what you would do differently. After a year you will have a field guide to your own patch that no one else has.

3. Pack for the conditions, not the catalogue

Carry less than you think. A camera, one or two lenses, spare batteries (cold drains them fast), a microfibre cloth, a head torch and something to kneel or lie on. A small waterproof bag or a shower cap over the camera is enough for most rain. If you are heading out before dawn, tell someone where you are going.

In the field

4. Arrive early and stay late

Arrive before the light you want, so you are set up and calm when it comes. Then stay after you think it is over. Some of the best colour in the sky appears fifteen to twenty minutes after sunset, after most people have packed up, and the soft blue light before sunrise suits water, snow and mist beautifully.

5. Walk slowly and look at the edges

In nature, interesting things happen at boundaries: where a wood meets a field, where a river meets the sea, where shade meets sun. Animals use edges to move and feed, and the light changes most quickly there. Walk them slowly, stop often, and look behind you as much as ahead.

6. Let animals come to you

With wildlife, the photographer who stays still usually does better than the one who walks around. Find a spot with cover and a clear view of a feeding area or path, sit down, and wait. Twenty minutes of stillness changes how animals treat you. If an animal changes what it is doing because of you, stops feeding, stares, or moves away, you are too close. Our guide to wildlife photography for beginners covers fieldcraft in much more depth.

7. Shoot the weather you get

Grey days are not bad days. Thin cloud acts like a huge softbox: it removes harsh shadows, which suits forests, waterfalls, fungi and flowers. Fog simplifies busy scenes into shapes. Rain saturates colour in leaves and rock. Wind is the only weather that genuinely makes most nature photography harder, and even then you can photograph moving grass and trees with slow shutter speeds instead of fighting it.

Composition

8. Decide what the picture is about

Before you lift the camera, try to name the subject in a few words: "the lone birch", "the heron's reflection", "the curve of the river". If you can't, the photograph probably won't be able to either. Once you know what it is about, every other decision gets easier: what to include, what to crop, where to focus.

9. Simplify the frame

Look at the edges of the viewfinder, not just the centre. Bright patches of sky, stray branches and half-cut objects at the edge pull the eye out of the picture. Step left or right, crouch, or zoom in until the frame contains only what supports the subject. Often the best fix is to take one step closer.

10. Change your height

Most people photograph everything from standing eye level, which is why so many nature photos look alike. Lie down to photograph a duck at water level and the background turns into a soft blur. Crouch for flowers. Climb a bank to look down on patterns in a river. Height is free and changes a picture more than any lens.

11. Build depth with foreground, middle and background

Wide scenes look flat when everything is far away. Find something close to the lens, a rock, a tuft of heather, a puddle with a reflection, and let the eye travel from there into the distance. Our landscape photography tips go into foregrounds, leading lines and scale.

12. Use the rule of thirds as a starting point, not a law

Placing the subject a third of the way across the frame usually feels more natural than dead centre, and leaving space in front of an animal's gaze gives it somewhere to look. But symmetry works brilliantly for reflections, and a centred subject in a lot of empty space can feel calm and deliberate. Use the grid in your viewfinder, then break it on purpose.

13. Look for small subjects when big ones fail

When the big view is dull, turn round and look down. Dew on leaves, frost patterns, lichen, beetles, the texture of bark. These subjects work in flat light and they are rarely photographed well, which makes them good practice for seeing composition in small spaces.

Camera settings that matter

You do not need to master every menu. You need a few defaults you trust and the habit of checking them.

14. Shoot RAW

A RAW file keeps far more information in the highlights and shadows than a JPEG, which is exactly what you need when a bright sky and a dark wood share the same frame. It also lets you set white balance afterwards. Phones offer RAW too on many models.

15. Protect the shutter speed first

Blur from a slow shutter speed can't be fixed. Noise from a high ISO mostly can. For moving animals set the shutter speed you need, and let auto ISO rise to meet it. For handheld scenes, stay at or above one over the focal length (1/200 s at 200 mm), and faster on high-resolution sensors.

16. Focus on the eye, or on purpose

With animals, a sharp eye makes the whole picture feel sharp, even if the rest is soft. Use a single focus point or eye detection and put it on the nearest eye. With landscapes, focus about a third of the way into the scene, or on your foreground subject, and check the result by zooming in on the screen.

17. Watch the histogram, not the screen

Screens lie, especially in bright sun or at night. The histogram tells you whether you have clipped the highlights (a spike against the right edge) or the shadows (against the left). For most nature scenes, expose so the brightest important area sits just short of the right edge.

After the shoot

18. Be ruthless when you choose

Go through the pictures the next day, not that night, when you still remember how it felt to be there. Ask whether the photograph works for someone who wasn't. Keep the best one from each scene, not five similar versions. Sharing three strong pictures does more for your work than sharing thirty good ones.

19. Edit for light, not for colour

Most nature photos are improved by small changes to exposure, contrast and white balance, and harmed by heavy saturation. Start with the basics: straighten the horizon, crop distractions, lift shadows a little, bring highlights down. If you add anything, add local contrast to the subject. If a green looks radioactive, it is too much.

20. Caption honestly

If an animal was captive, baited or photographed at a game farm, say so. If a picture is a composite or the sky was replaced, say so. Honest captions protect your credibility and protect wildlife from the pressure that faked "wild" photos create. The North American Nature Photography Association's ethics guidance are a good standard to follow.

Ethics, the tip that comes first

21. Leave every place as you found it

Stay on paths where they exist, keep dogs on leads near ground-nesting birds and livestock, don't move nests or vegetation to get a cleaner shot, and don't share exact locations of sensitive species online. Follow the Countryside Code in the UK, or the Leave No Trace principles elsewhere. Disturbing nesting birds can be a criminal offence in many countries, including the UK.

It is a good rule of thumb that the animal's day matters more than your picture. If you only take one habit from this list, take that one. The rest will follow.

Questions people ask

What is the best camera setting for nature photography?

There isn't one, but a good default is aperture priority at f/8 for scenes and f/4–f/5.6 for animals, auto ISO with a sensible minimum shutter speed (1/500 s for animals, 1/60 s or a tripod for still scenes), and RAW files. Change it when the subject tells you to.

Do I need an expensive camera for nature photography?

No. Light, timing and composition matter far more. A ten-year-old camera or a recent phone will make strong landscape and close-up pictures. Long lenses matter mostly for small or shy wildlife.

What time of day is best for nature photography?

The hour or two after sunrise and before sunset, when light is low, warm and directional. Overcast days are excellent for forests, waterfalls and flowers because the cloud softens contrast.

How do I make nature photos look less flat?

Use side light or backlight instead of the sun behind you, include a foreground, middle ground and background, and look for a single clear subject. In editing, add a little local contrast rather than global saturation.
