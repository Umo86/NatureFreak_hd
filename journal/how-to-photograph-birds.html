How to photograph birds: a complete beginner's guide

Birds are the most accessible wild animals on the planet and some of the hardest to photograph well. Here is how to close the distance, freeze the action and keep the bird's welfare first.

By the NATUREFreak teamUpdated 13 November 20266 min read

The short version

  • Reach matters. Aim for 400–600 mm on full frame, or the equivalent on a crop sensor.
  • Start with 1/1000 s, the widest aperture, auto ISO and continuous autofocus on the eye.
  • Learn behaviour, then position yourself where the bird will come, rather than walking towards it.
  • A garden feeder with a natural perch beside it is the easiest bird hide there is.
  • Never use call playback, flush birds or approach nests. The bird always comes first.

Birds are everywhere. They're in city centres, on motorway verges, in back gardens and on every coastline. That makes them the best possible subject for learning wildlife photography, and also one of the hardest to do well. They're small, quick, wary, and they're often against a bright sky or deep in a hedge.

This guide is for anyone who has tried to photograph birds and come home with tiny brown shapes. It covers equipment, settings, fieldcraft and a simple garden set-up, with ethics running all the way through.

What you need

A long lens

The single most important piece of equipment is focal length. A bird that fills your field of view at 600 mm is a speck at 200 mm, and cropping can only rescue so much. As a rough guide:

  • Garden birds at a feeder 3–5 metres away: 300 mm is often enough.
  • Birds in parks, reserves and hides: 400 mm minimum, 600 mm comfortable.
  • Shy birds, waders and birds in flight: 500–800 mm.

Crop-sensor cameras multiply the effective reach (about 1.5x on APS-C, 2x on Micro Four Thirds), which is why they are popular for birds. Affordable long zooms such as 150–600 mm and 200–600 mm models made bird photography far more accessible; a used one is a very good first purchase.

A camera that can track

Any recent mirrorless camera or a mid-range DSLR will work. What helps most is bird-eye detection autofocus, which is now on many models, and a burst rate of at least 7–10 frames per second.

Binoculars and an ID app

You'll find more birds with binoculars than with a camera to your eye. Free apps like Merlin Bird ID identify birds by sound and photo, and eBird shows recent sightings near you, which is invaluable for finding particular species.

Settings for bird photography

Birds rarely keep still, and light is often uneven, so the most reliable starting point is manual mode with auto ISO.

Situation Shutter speed Aperture Focus
Perched bird, still 1/500–1/1000 s Wide open (f/5.6–f/6.3) Single point or eye detection on the eye
Small active birds (tits, warblers) 1/1600–1/2500 s Wide open Eye detection, small zone
Birds in flight 1/2000–1/4000 s Wide open Wide zone or tracking
Wing blur, creative 1/30–1/125 s f/8 Continuous, pan with the bird

Set ISO to auto with a maximum around 6400 to 12,800, and use exposure compensation to correct for the scene. White birds (egrets, swans, gulls) are easily overexposed; dial in −0.7 to −1. Dark birds against a bright sky need +1 or more.

Shoot RAW, use continuous autofocus (AF-C or AI Servo), and burst mode. Birds blink, turn their heads and shift weight constantly; a short burst gives you the frame where everything lines up.

Fieldcraft: let the bird come to you

The fastest way to fail at bird photography is to walk towards birds. They see you long before you see them, and a bird that's been flushed has wasted energy it needs. These approaches work better.

Learn the bird's routine. Kingfishers use the same perches over a stretch of river. Herons fish the same shallows. Waders follow the tide, feeding as it drops and gathering at roosts at high tide. Robins and blackbirds patrol the same lawns. Once you know where a bird will be, you can be there first.

Sit and wait. Find cover facing a feeding area and stay still. Ten minutes is the minimum; an hour is better.

Use hides and vehicles. Public hides on reserves put you close to birds used to people. A car is an excellent hide; many birds ignore vehicles.

Approach slowly and indirectly. If you have to move, go at an angle, keep low, and stop every time the bird looks up. If it starts to look alarmed, back off.

Get low. Photographing a wader at water level, lying on a mat, makes the background fall into a smooth wash and puts the viewer in the bird's world.

A simple garden set-up

A feeder in the garden turns your house into a hide and gives you dozens of chances a day. Done well, it also helps the birds.

  1. Place a feeder a few metres from a window or a spot where you can sit hidden.
  2. Add a natural perch (a mossy branch, a twig of hawthorn) 30–60 cm from the feeder. Birds land on it before hopping across, and pictures on a branch look far better than pictures on a plastic feeder.
  3. Think about the background. Position the perch so the background is several metres away: a hedge or a lawn in shade. It will blur into a smooth wash at wide apertures.
  4. Mind the light. Morning or evening sun from beside or behind you works best. Overcast days give soft, even light.
  5. Keep it clean. Clean feeders and water regularly to avoid spreading disease, and keep them away from where cats can ambush birds.

You can shoot from inside through an open window, or from a small pop-up hide in the garden. Shooting through glass is possible but softens images; open the window if you can.

Composition

Eye contact and catchlight. A small highlight in the bird's eye brings a portrait to life. Position yourself so the light source is behind you or to one side.

Clean backgrounds. Move left or right to put a distant, even background behind the bird. Distance between the bird and the background matters more than aperture.

Room to look into. Place the bird so it looks into the frame, with more space in front of it than behind.

Habitat. Not every frame needs to be tight. A small bird in a large frame of reeds or snow tells a story about where it lives.

Action. Feeding, bathing, preening, singing, squabbling, landing. Watch for the moments just before action, like a bird crouching before take-off, and be ready.

Ethics: non-negotiable

Bird photography has a real problem with disturbance, especially since social media. These rules, drawn from Audubon's ethical guidelines and UK law, should be absolute.

  • No call playback. Recorded calls can lure birds away from nests and young and stress territorial birds.
  • Stay away from nests. In the UK it's an offence to intentionally disturb many species at or near a nest without a licence (RSPB). Elsewhere, the principle is the same: if a bird is nesting, give it space.
  • Never flush birds for a flight shot, and never cut vegetation to improve a view.
  • Be careful with locations. Rare birds and owl roosts can attract crowds that cause real harm. Share a general area, not a precise spot.
  • Caption honestly. If a bird was captive or baited, say so.
  • Watch the bird, not the screen. If it changes its behaviour because of you, you're too close.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

Soft pictures. Usually shutter speed too slow or focus on the wrong spot. Go faster and put the focus point on the eye.

Tiny birds in big frames. You need more reach or need to get closer through fieldcraft, not by walking towards the bird.

Busy backgrounds. Move your position, not the bird's. A small step can change the background completely.

Burned-out white birds. Use negative exposure compensation and check the histogram.

Pictures of the back of a bird. Wait. Birds turn. Patience gives you the head turn and the catchlight.

Questions people ask

What lens do I need for bird photography?

Ideally 400–600 mm on a full-frame camera. Popular options are 100–400 mm, 150–600 mm and 200–600 mm zooms, or a 300 mm on a crop-sensor camera. For garden birds at a feeder a few metres away, a 70–300 mm can work well.

What are the best camera settings for bird photography?

For perched birds: manual with auto ISO, the widest aperture, 1/500–1/1000 s, continuous autofocus with bird-eye detection or a single point, and burst mode. For birds in flight, go to 1/2000–1/3200 s and use a larger focus area.

Is it OK to use bird calls to attract birds for photography?

Generally no. Playing recorded calls can pull birds away from nests and feeding, and can stress territorial birds. Audubon's ethical guidelines advise against playback for photography.

How do I get closer to birds without scaring them?

Let them come to you. Sit still near feeding areas, use hides and cars, approach slowly at an angle and stop when the bird looks at you. Learn its habits so you can be in place before it arrives.
