Wildlife photography for beginners: gear, settings and fieldcraft

You don't need a safari or a two-metre lens to start. You need a place you can visit often, a few reliable settings, and the patience to let animals forget you are there.

By the NATUREFreak teamUpdated 13 November 20268 min read

The short version

  • Start with common animals near home. Gulls, deer, squirrels and garden birds teach every skill you need.
  • A 100–400 mm or 150–600 mm zoom on any recent camera is plenty; a 70–300 mm is a fine first lens.
  • Begin with manual mode, a fast shutter speed (1/1000 s), the widest aperture and auto ISO.
  • Get low, focus on the eye, and wait for behaviour rather than a static portrait.
  • Fieldcraft and ethics come before pictures. If the animal reacts to you, back off.

Wildlife photography has a reputation for being expensive and exotic. Look at the winners of any major competition and you'll see snow leopards, orcas and cheetahs. What you won't see is the years of practice on pigeons, robins and park squirrels that came first, and that is where nearly every good wildlife photographer started.

This guide covers the three things that matter for a beginner, in order of importance: fieldcraft (how to find animals and get close without disturbing them), settings (how to make the camera keep up), and gear (what is actually worth buying).

Start with what lives near you

The best first subjects are common, bold and close to home. Ducks and swans on a park lake. Gulls on a harbour wall. Grey squirrels, rabbits, deer in a country park, garden birds at a feeder. They let you practise focusing, tracking and composing a hundred times in an afternoon, and nothing is lost if you get it wrong.

Common doesn't mean dull. A mallard lit from behind at sunrise, a gull shaking off spray, a squirrel in falling snow: these can be striking pictures. The skill is the same one you'll need later for a kingfisher or a lion, and you'll have learned it before the stakes are high.

Fieldcraft: how to get closer

Lenses help, but fieldcraft decides whether you see the animal at all. These habits matter more than anything you can buy.

Go at the right time. Most mammals and birds are most active in the first hours after dawn and the last hours before dusk. That happens to be the best light, too.

Learn the animal. Read about where it feeds, rests and travels, and what disturbs it. Deer follow the same paths at the same times; herons return to the same fishing spots; foxes cross field edges. A morning reading about a species saves days in the field.

Use the wind. Mammals rely on scent. Approach with the wind blowing from the animal towards you, not the other way round.

Move slowly and indirectly. Walking straight at an animal reads as a threat. Approach at an angle, stop often, and avoid looking straight at it. When it looks up, freeze.

Break your outline. Muted clothing, no rustling waterproofs, and staying low against a bank or hedge make a big difference. You don't need full camouflage; you need to not look like a person walking towards it.

Use hides and cars. Many nature reserves have public hides where birds are used to people. A car is an excellent mobile hide: animals often ignore vehicles that would flee from someone on foot. Turn the engine off and rest the lens on a beanbag on the window.

Sit and wait. The most effective technique of all. Find somewhere with cover overlooking a place animals use, and stay put for an hour. Within twenty minutes the wood starts to carry on as if you were not there.

The one rule

If the animal changes its behaviour because of you, you are too close. Stopping feeding, staring at you, alarm calls, a raised head, moving away: all of these mean back off. Around nests and dens, stay well away; disturbing nesting birds is illegal in the UK and many other countries (RSPB guidance). In parks, follow the posted distances. In Yellowstone, for example, the rule is 100 yards from bears and wolves and 25 yards from everything else.

The settings to start with

Wildlife moves unpredictably and often in poor light, so the goal is a set-up that keeps the shutter speed fast and lets the camera handle brightness.

Manual mode with auto ISO

This is the most useful beginner set-up for animals, and it's simpler than it sounds. You choose the aperture and shutter speed; the camera adjusts ISO to get the exposure right.

  • Aperture: as wide as your lens goes (f/5.6 or f/6.3 on most zooms). This lets in the most light and blurs the background.
  • Shutter speed: 1/1000 s as a default. Drop to 1/250–1/500 s for a resting animal, go to 1/2000 s or faster for running animals and birds in flight.
  • ISO: auto, with a maximum you're happy with. On modern cameras, ISO 6400 is usually fine and noise reduction software handles more.
  • Exposure compensation: use it to brighten or darken. Dark animals against bright snow need +1 or so; a white egret against dark water may need −1.

If manual with auto ISO feels too much, aperture priority with a minimum shutter speed set in the auto ISO menu does nearly the same job.

Focus and drive

  • Continuous autofocus (AF-C on Nikon, Fuji and Sony; AI Servo on Canon) so the camera keeps tracking a moving subject.
  • Animal eye detection if your camera has it. If not, a single small focus point placed on the eye.
  • Back-button focus is worth learning once you're comfortable. It separates focusing from the shutter release, so you can hold focus on a still animal and react instantly when it moves.
  • Burst mode at a moderate rate. Short bursts at the peak of action give you a choice of wing or leg positions.

Shoot RAW

Animals often appear against bright skies or in deep shade. RAW files give you room to recover highlights and lift shadows that a JPEG would have lost.

Composition: making it more than a record shot

A sharp, well-exposed animal in the centre of the frame is a record shot. To make a photograph, think about the following.

Eye level. Get down to the animal's height. Lying on the ground to photograph a duck changes everything: the background melts into colour, and the viewer meets the animal as an equal.

Background. Move a few steps so the background is far away and uncluttered: distant trees, water, sky. A clean background makes an ordinary subject look professional.

Space to move. Leave room in front of the animal's face or direction of travel. An animal looking out of the frame feels cramped.

Behaviour. A portrait is fine; a moment is better. Feeding, preening, yawning, interacting, shaking off water. Wait for it. Wildlife photography is mostly waiting, and the waiting is the job.

Animal in its world. Not every shot needs to be frame-filling. A small deer in a vast misty field can say more than a tight head shot.

Gear: what's worth buying

You can start wildlife photography with whatever camera you have. When you are ready to spend money, spend it in this order.

1. A telephoto lens

Reach matters more than the camera body. Rough guide:

Lens Good for Notes
70–300 mm Larger animals, parks, zoos, deer Light and affordable; a fine first lens
100–400 mm Most wildlife The sweet spot for many people
150–600 mm / 200–600 mm Birds and shy mammals Heavier, but huge reach for the money
300 mm f/4 or 500 mm f/5.6 primes Birds, serious use Sharp and light, but less flexible

APS-C and Micro Four Thirds cameras crop the image, which makes any lens behave as if it were longer (about 1.5x on APS-C and 2x on Micro Four Thirds). That's an advantage for wildlife.

2. A camera with good tracking autofocus

Any mirrorless or DSLR body from the last few years will do. The features that help most are subject detection (animals and birds), fast continuous autofocus and a decent burst rate. You don't need the newest model; a used, two-generation-old body with a long lens beats a new body with a short one.

3. Support

A beanbag for car windows and hide walls, and a monopod for long walks with a heavy lens. A tripod with a gimbal head is useful for long waits in one place, but not essential to start.

4. Practical bits

Fast memory cards (bursts fill buffers quickly), spare batteries, a rain cover, and drab clothing that doesn't rustle. A pair of binoculars is underrated: you'll find far more animals with them than with your eye.

Where to practise

  • Nature reserves with hides. RSPB and Wildlife Trust reserves in the UK, or national wildlife refuges in the US, have hides where birds are used to people.
  • Deer parks. Richmond Park and Bushy Park in London, or Knepp, Studley Royal and Bradgate Park elsewhere in England, have deer that tolerate people at a respectful distance. Avoid the autumn rut at close range; stags are dangerous then.
  • Coasts and estuaries. Wading birds, seals and seabirds, often with clean water backgrounds.
  • Your garden. A feeder by a window turns your house into a hide. See our guide to how to photograph birds for a simple set-up.

Common beginner mistakes

  1. Shutter speed too slow. The most common cause of soft wildlife pictures. If in doubt, go faster. Our five ways to get sharper wildlife photos covers the rest.
  2. Standing up. Photographing from human height makes animals look small and the background busy.
  3. Chasing. Following an animal makes it move away and gives you pictures of its back. Let it come to you.
  4. Cutting off feet, tails and wingtips. Leave a little room around the animal.
  5. Shooting only portraits. Try wide shots that show habitat, and abstract details like feathers or fur.
  6. Over-editing. Crushing blacks and adding saturation makes fur and feathers look plastic.

When you're ready for more

Once you can reliably photograph birds at a feeder and deer in a park, try something faster: birds in flight are the classic test. When you're ready to travel, read our guides to safari photography and photographing polar bears, and spend an evening looking through the Wildlife Photographer of the Year gallery. Then take what you notice back to your local patch.

Questions people ask

What lens should a beginner buy for wildlife photography?

A telephoto zoom that reaches at least 300 mm, and ideally 400–600 mm on a full-frame camera (or 300–400 mm on APS-C, which crops the view by about 1.5x). Popular first choices are 70–300 mm, 100–400 mm and 150–600 mm zooms. Buying used is a sensible way to get more reach for your money.

What are good camera settings for wildlife photography?

Start in manual mode with auto ISO: aperture wide open (f/5.6–f/6.3 on most zooms), shutter speed 1/1000 s for animals that move, 1/250–1/500 s for still ones, continuous autofocus, a single point or animal-eye detection, and burst mode.

Can you do wildlife photography with a phone?

For large or tame animals at close range, yes, and phones are excellent for animals in their wider habitat. For small or shy wildlife at a distance you need optical zoom; digital zoom on a phone quickly loses detail.

How close should you get to wild animals?

Close enough that the animal carries on behaving normally, and no closer. Many parks set legal minimums: Yellowstone, for example, requires 100 yards from bears and wolves and 25 yards from other wildlife.
