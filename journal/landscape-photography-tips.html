Landscape photography tips: 17 lessons from the field

A great landscape photograph is rarely about the place. It's about being there at the right moment, standing in the right spot, and knowing what to leave out.

By the NATUREFreak teamUpdated 13 November 20266 min read

The short version

  • Light and weather make the picture. Plan for sunrise, sunset and changing conditions.
  • Every strong landscape has a subject. Find it before you set up the tripod.
  • Foregrounds create depth; telephoto lenses create compression. Use both.
  • A tripod, a polariser and patience will do more than a new camera.
  • Edit lightly. If a viewer notices your editing before your scene, it's too much.

If you have ever stood in front of a magnificent view, taken a photograph, and found it flat and small on the screen at home, you already know the main problem in landscape photography. The eye does a lot of work that a camera doesn't. It moves around a scene, ignores the bin by the car park, adjusts to bright sky and dark rock at once, and adds feeling. A camera records everything equally.

These lessons are about closing that gap. They are not ordered by importance; read them all, then go back to the ones that sting.

Light and timing

1. The light is the subject as much as the land

Landscape photography is really photography of light falling on land. At midday, sun from overhead flattens hills and bleaches colour. In the first and last hours of the day, low light rakes across the ground, picks out texture and throws long shadows that show shape. Learn where the sun rises and sets at your location in each season; apps such as PhotoPills and The Photographer's Ephemeris show this on a map.

2. Shoot in the blue hour too

Before sunrise and after sunset there is a period when the sky is bright enough to light the land softly and evenly, with a cool blue cast. It suits water, snow, coastlines and towns with lights. Most people arrive for sunrise and leave after sunset; the blue hour either side is where calm, unusual pictures come from.

3. Bad weather is good weather

A clear blue sky is pleasant to walk under and dull to photograph. Clouds give a sky shape and colour. Fog hides clutter and turns hills into layers. Showers bring rainbows and dramatic breaks of light. Snow simplifies everything. The best landscape photographers watch forecasts for change: a clearing storm, mist after a cold clear night, a front passing at dawn.

4. Go back

The first visit to a place is reconnaissance. On the second you know where to stand. On the tenth you know which morning, which tide and which wind direction the scene needs. Iconic shots are rarely taken on a first attempt.

Composition

5. Find the subject first

"A nice view" isn't a subject. A lone tree, a bend in a river, a lit ridge, a lighthouse, a path through heather: these are subjects. Decide what yours is, then build the frame around it. If you can't name the subject, you're probably photographing a view, and views rarely make strong pictures.

6. Use a foreground to pull the viewer in

With a wide lens, put something interesting close to the camera, a pattern of rocks, flowers, ice, ripples in sand, and get low so it fills the bottom third of the frame. The eye enters the picture there and travels back to the main subject. Without a foreground, wide-angle landscapes often look empty.

7. Lead with lines

Rivers, walls, paths, tide lines and ridges guide the eye. The strongest lines start near a corner of the frame and lead towards the subject. Curved lines (an S-shaped river) feel calmer than straight ones.

8. Give it scale

Big landscapes can look small in photographs because there's nothing to measure them against. A person, a tree, a car or a building in the distance tells the viewer how vast the scene is. A tiny figure on a ridge is a classic for a reason.

9. Use a telephoto lens more than you think

Wide lenses exaggerate distance and shrink mountains. A telephoto (70–200 mm or longer) does the opposite: it compresses layers of hills, makes distant peaks loom and picks out patterns that a wide lens loses. On misty mornings, long lenses turn ridgelines into graphic layers.

10. Watch the horizon

Keep it level (use the electronic level in your camera) and decide where it goes. A dramatic sky deserves two thirds of the frame; an interesting foreground deserves two thirds the other way. A horizon cutting the frame in half usually only works with reflections.

Technique

11. Use a tripod and slow down

A tripod's biggest benefit isn't sharpness; it's that it makes you slow down and refine the composition. It also lets you use base ISO and a small aperture in dim light and make long exposures. A light travel tripod you'll actually carry is better than a heavy one left in the car.

12. Get everything sharp, deliberately

For front-to-back sharpness, use f/8 to f/11 and focus about a third of the way into the scene, or on your foreground subject if it's close. Going beyond f/16 softens the whole image through diffraction. For a foreground very close to the lens, take a few frames focused at different distances and blend them later (focus stacking). The details are in our camera settings guide for landscape photography.

13. Learn three filters, ignore the rest

  • Circular polariser: cuts glare from water and wet leaves, deepens blue skies and saturates foliage. The one filter worth owning first. Be careful with wide lenses, where it can darken one part of the sky unevenly.
  • Neutral density (ND): darkens the whole scene so you can use long shutter speeds for smooth water and streaking clouds. A 6-stop and a 10-stop cover most needs.
  • Graduated ND: darkens the sky while leaving the land alone. Many photographers now use exposure bracketing instead.

14. Bracket when the range is too wide

When the sky is far brighter than the land, take three to five frames at different exposures and blend them. Modern sensors handle a lot of range in one RAW file, but bracketing is cheap insurance at sunrise and sunset.

15. Use slow shutter speeds with intent

Half a second makes a waterfall smooth but keeps texture. Several seconds turns waves into mist. Minutes turn clouds into streaks. Choose the effect, then the speed. No camera? An iPhone can do a convincing version with Live Photos; see our iPhone long exposure guide.

Editing

16. Edit for what you saw, not what you wish you'd seen

Start with white balance, exposure and highlights and shadows. Then straighten and crop. Then add a little local contrast and use gradients or masks to balance a bright sky. Avoid global saturation sliders; vibrance is gentler. If you replace a sky or blend images from different times, say so in your caption, especially for competitions, which often ban it.

17. Print your best pictures

A print shows faults a screen hides: haloes, noise, over-sharpening, colour that's too strong. It also shows what's genuinely good. Print one picture a month, small, and live with it for a while. You'll learn more from that than from any number of likes.

A simple plan for your next outing

  1. Check the forecast for change (cloud, mist, clearing rain) and the sunrise or sunset time and direction.
  2. Pick a location you can reach comfortably before the light, ideally one you've scouted.
  3. Arrive 45 minutes early. Walk around. Find a subject and a foreground.
  4. Set up the tripod, level the horizon, set f/8–f/11, base ISO, and focus a third of the way in.
  5. Shoot as the light develops, and keep shooting through the blue hour.
  6. Before you leave, take one picture with a telephoto, one with a person for scale and one you wouldn't normally take.

Questions people ask

What is the best lens for landscape photography?

There isn't a single best lens. A standard zoom (24–70 mm or 24–105 mm on full frame) covers most scenes. A wide angle (14–24 mm or 16–35 mm) suits big foregrounds and night skies, and a 70–200 mm telephoto is excellent for picking out details and layered hills.

Do I need a tripod for landscape photography?

Not always, but it helps a lot. A tripod lets you use a low ISO and small aperture in dim light, slow down your composition, and make long exposures of water and clouds.

What time is best for landscape photography?

The golden hours around sunrise and sunset, and the blue hour just before sunrise and after sunset. Stormy, foggy and changeable weather often produces more dramatic pictures than clear skies.

How do you make landscape photos look sharp from front to back?

Use an aperture around f/8 to f/11, focus about a third of the way into the scene or on the foreground subject, and use a tripod. For very close foregrounds, take several frames focused at different distances and blend them (focus stacking).
