Best camera settings for birds in flight

Sharp birds in flight come from three things working together: a fast enough shutter, autofocus that's set up to track, and a photographer who found the bird before it reached the frame.

By the NATUREFreak teamUpdated 13 November 20266 min read

The short version

  • Starting point: manual mode, 1/2000 s, widest aperture, auto ISO, continuous AF, burst.
  • Small fast birds need 1/3200–1/4000 s; large soaring birds can be sharp at 1/1250 s.
  • Use a zone or tracking AF area to acquire, with bird-eye detection if your camera has it.
  • Add +0.7 to +1.3 EV for birds against bright sky; −0.7 for white birds against dark backgrounds.
  • Find the bird with both eyes open, pre-focus at the right distance, and pan smoothly through the shot.

Photographing a bird in flight is the moment many wildlife photographers realise how much their camera can do, and how much it needs their help. A gull wheeling over a harbour or a heron lifting off from a river will expose every weak setting and every hesitation.

The good news is that once your camera is set up properly, birds in flight are mostly a matter of practice. This guide gives you the set-up, then the technique.

If you're still getting comfortable with perched birds, start with our guide to how to photograph birds and come back.

The starting set-up

This works on almost any camera made in the last decade. Set it once, save it to a custom mode (C1, U1 or a recall button), and you'll always have it ready.

Setting Value
Mode Manual (M)
Shutter speed 1/2000 s
Aperture Widest available (often f/5.6–f/6.3)
ISO Auto, max 6400–12,800
Exposure compensation +0.7 EV against sky (adjust per scene)
Focus mode Continuous (AF-C / AI Servo)
AF area Zone or wide tracking; bird-eye detection on
Drive High-speed burst
Image stabilisation On (sport or mode 2/3 if available)
File type RAW
Shutter type Mechanical or a fast-readout electronic shutter

Why manual with auto ISO

In flight, birds pass from bright sky to dark trees and back in a second. If you fix both shutter speed and aperture, the camera can't change either to keep up; auto ISO absorbs the change instead. You get the motion-freezing speed you chose and a correct exposure, at the cost of some noise in poor light, which modern software handles well.

Shutter speed by bird

The faster and smaller the bird, the faster the shutter. Wingtips move much faster than bodies, so a body can be sharp while wingtips blur; decide whether that bothers you.

Bird type Examples Starting shutter speed
Large, slow wingbeat Herons, eagles, swans, geese, pelicans 1/1250–1/2000 s
Medium Gulls, ducks, kites, buzzards, owls 1/2000–1/2500 s
Fast, agile Terns, falcons, kingfishers, puffins 1/3200–1/4000 s
Small, erratic Swallows, martins, warblers 1/4000 s and faster
Hovering Kestrels, hummingbirds 1/3200 s+ (or slow for wing blur)

In poor light, it's better to accept a higher ISO than a slower shutter. If you have to slow down, choose moments when the bird is gliding or at the top of a wingbeat.

Autofocus: the part that matters most

Shutter speed freezes motion, but only if the camera focused on the bird. Most missed flight shots are focus failures.

Continuous autofocus

AF-C (Nikon, Sony, Fujifilm, OM System) or AI Servo (Canon) keeps adjusting focus while you half-press or hold the AF button. Single AF won't work for moving birds.

Choosing the AF area

  • Wide or full tracking: best against clean sky. The camera finds the bird anywhere in the frame.
  • Zone or large expanded area: best against cluttered backgrounds (trees, hills, water). A smaller area makes the camera less likely to grab the background.
  • Single point: precise but hard to keep on a moving bird; useful for slow, large birds when other modes jump.

Subject detection

If your camera has bird or animal eye detection, turn it on. It works best when you've already acquired the bird with a zone, then lets the camera follow the eye.

Tracking sensitivity

Most cameras let you set how quickly autofocus moves to a new subject. For birds against clean sky, a quicker response helps; for birds passing behind branches, a slower ("locked-on") setting stops the camera jumping to the branch.

Back-button focus

Moving autofocus to a rear button (AF-ON) separates focusing from the shutter. You can keep tracking without firing, fire without refocusing, and switch between two AF areas on two buttons. It takes a week to get used to and is worth it.

Exposure: sky fools the meter

A bird against a bright sky is the classic exposure trap. The meter sees a lot of bright sky and darkens everything, leaving the bird as a silhouette.

  • Dark or mid-toned bird against sky: +0.7 to +1.3 EV.
  • White bird against sky: around 0 to +0.3 EV; check that white feathers aren't clipping.
  • White bird against dark water or trees: −0.7 to −1 EV.
  • Any bird against similar-toned background: 0 EV.

Check the histogram and highlight warnings after a few frames, and adjust. Underside shadows can be lifted in editing; clipped white feathers can't.

Technique: finding and following

The settings get you halfway. The rest is handling.

Find the bird early. Pick it up in the distance with both eyes open, then bring the camera up. Trying to find a bird through a long lens is slow.

Pre-focus. If you know roughly where the bird will be (a perch it returns to, a flight path along a river), focus on that distance in advance so the lens doesn't hunt.

Stance. Feet shoulder-width apart, elbows tucked in, rotate from the hips rather than the arms. With a heavy lens, a monopod or a gimbal head on a tripod helps.

Pan smoothly and follow through. Start tracking before you press the shutter, keep panning while you shoot, and keep going after you stop. Stopping as you press is what blurs the shot.

Short bursts. Fire bursts of a few frames as the bird reaches the best position, rather than holding the button down for seconds and filling the buffer at the crucial moment.

Light behind you, wind in your face. Birds take off and land into the wind. Standing with the wind at your back means birds come towards you; with the sun behind you as well, their faces are lit.

Composition in flight

  • Wing position matters. Wings fully up or fully down usually look better than mid-stroke. Bursts give you the choice.
  • Leave space ahead. Give the bird room to fly into.
  • Eye contact. A bird banking towards you, head turned, with light in its eye, beats a bird flying away.
  • Don't clip wingtips. Zoom out a little; you can crop later.
  • Try slow shutter speeds too. At 1/30–1/60 s, panning with a gull or swan creates painterly wing blur with a relatively sharp head.

Where and how to practise

Practise on common birds that fly predictably and often:

  • Gulls at harbours and beaches. Endless, patient practice.
  • Ducks, geese and swans on park lakes, especially landing and taking off.
  • Pigeons and corvids in towns.
  • Red kites and buzzards in many parts of the UK; kites often circle low over fields and roads.
  • Gannet and puffin colonies in summer, from public viewpoints, keeping well back from cliff edges and burrows.

Spend an hour photographing gulls once a week and your success rate will rise quickly. Keep the rate expectations realistic: even experienced photographers keep only a small fraction of flight frames.

Ethics in flight photography

Never flush birds to get them flying, whether by walking at them, throwing food or using playback. Don't approach nesting colonies so closely that birds leave nests. Take-off shots are wonderful when birds leave on their own; they're not worth causing. See Audubon's guidelines for more.

Quick troubleshooting

Problem Likely cause Fix
Whole bird soft AF missed or locked on background Smaller AF zone; acquire earlier; slower tracking sensitivity
Body sharp, wings blurred Shutter too slow for wings Go faster, or accept and time for top/bottom of wingbeat
Bird is a dark silhouette Meter fooled by bright sky +1 EV; check histogram
Very noisy images Light too poor for the shutter speed Shoot earlier/later in better light; denoise; accept slightly slower speed when gliding
Missed the moment Buffer full or late reaction Shorter bursts; pre-focus; keep both eyes open

Once your flight shots are reliably sharp, take the same settings on the road. On a safari, they work for lilac-breasted rollers and running cheetahs alike.

Questions people ask

What shutter speed do I need for birds in flight?

Start at 1/2000 s. Large birds such as herons, eagles and swans can be sharp from around 1/1250 s. Small, fast birds like swallows, terns and kingfishers usually need 1/3200 s or faster. For deliberate wing blur, drop to 1/30–1/125 s and pan.

Which autofocus mode is best for birds in flight?

Continuous autofocus (AF-C or AI Servo) with a zone or wide tracking area. Turn on bird or animal eye detection if your camera has it. Against clean skies a wide area works; against busy backgrounds a smaller zone helps the camera avoid locking on to trees.

Should I use manual or shutter priority for birds in flight?

Manual with auto ISO is the most popular choice: you fix the shutter speed and aperture, and the camera adjusts ISO as the bird moves between bright sky and darker backgrounds. Shutter priority with auto ISO works similarly.

Why are my birds in flight photos blurry?

The usual causes are a shutter speed that's too slow, autofocus that locked on to the background or a wingtip, or jerky panning. Increase the shutter speed, use continuous AF with a sensible area, and keep panning smoothly after you press the shutter.
