A long exposure photograph keeps the shutter open long enough for movement to blur while still things stay sharp. Waterfalls turn to silk, waves become mist, clouds stretch into streaks. On a traditional camera that means a tripod, a slow shutter speed and often a dark neutral density filter to stop the image overexposing in daylight.

An iPhone gets there by a different route. It doesn't hold its shutter open for seconds in daylight; it records a short burst of frames and blends them. The result can look remarkably like the real thing, provided you understand the method and work with it.

## How the iPhone's Long Exposure effect works

When **Live Photos** is on, your iPhone records about **1.5 seconds before and after** you press the shutter, roughly three seconds in total. In the Photos app you can turn that clip into a **Long Exposure**: the phone aligns the frames and averages them into a single picture. Anything that moved during those three seconds smears into a blur; anything that stayed put stays sharp.

That has two consequences:

1. **You get about three seconds of blur, no more.** That's plenty for waterfalls, rivers and waves, but not for streaking clouds.
2. **The phone itself must stay still.** If you move, everything moves, and the whole frame turns soft. The phone does some alignment, but stability is the difference between a good result and a mess.

The finished long exposure is also slightly lower resolution and cropped compared with the original photo, which is worth knowing if you plan to print large.

## Step by step: Live Photo to long exposure

### 1. Turn on Live Photos

Open the Camera app in Photo mode. Look for the **Live** icon (three concentric circles), usually at the top of the screen. If it has a line through it, tap it to turn Live on. To stop iOS switching it off between sessions, go to **Settings > Camera > Preserve Settings** and turn on **Live Photo**.

On iPhone Pro models, if **ProRAW** is enabled, Live Photos are unavailable. Switch to standard HEIF or JPEG for this technique.

### 2. Choose a scene with movement against something still

The effect works best when there is a clear contrast between motion and stillness:

- a waterfall or stream with rocks and moss
- waves washing over a rocky shore
- a river under a bridge
- grass or reeds blowing beside a fixed tree
- traffic at dusk on a road below you

Scenes where everything moves (a crowd, a windy forest) just look blurry.

### 3. Hold it absolutely still

This is the step that decides everything.

- **Use a tripod** if you have one; a small phone tripod or a clamp weighs almost nothing.
- **Or brace it** on a wall, a fence post, a rock or your bag. Even a hand pressed flat against a tree is steadier than holding it in the air.
- **Use the self-timer** (3 seconds) so pressing the button doesn't shake the phone. A remote shutter or an Apple Watch also works.
- **Keep it still for the whole clip**, including the 1.5 seconds after the shot.

### 4. Take the photo

Frame, tap on the most important still object to set focus and exposure, then drag the exposure slider (the sun icon) down a little. Moving water is often the brightest part of the scene and blurs into a pale wash; a slightly darker exposure keeps its texture.

### 5. Convert it to a long exposure

1. Open the photo in the **Photos** app.
2. Tap the **Live** label in the top-left corner.
3. Choose **Long Exposure**.

The phone takes a moment to process. On older iOS versions, swipe up on the photo and choose Long Exposure from the Effects row. To undo, tap the label again and choose **Live**.

You can also do this to Live Photos you took months ago, so it's worth scrolling back through any waterfall or seaside photos in your library.

## Tips for better results

**Shoot several.** Three seconds of water doesn't always blur evenly. Take five or six Live Photos of the same scene and convert them all; one will be noticeably better.

**Get close and low.** The closer the phone is to moving water, the more the movement shows. A low position at the edge of a stream with wet rocks in the foreground works far better than a distant, high view.

**Overcast is better than sun.** Bright sun makes white water clip to pure white. Soft light keeps detail. Early morning and evening also work well.

**Use the ultra-wide carefully.** It gives dramatic foregrounds but makes distant waterfalls tiny. The main (1x) camera usually gives the cleanest result.

**Clean the lens.** Spray and mist settle on the lens near waterfalls and the sea. Wipe it before every shot.

**Watch for people.** Anyone walking through the frame becomes a ghost. Sometimes that's interesting; usually it isn't.

## Night mode is something different

People often assume **Night mode**, which can run for up to about 30 seconds on a tripod, is the iPhone's long-exposure mode. It isn't, quite.

Night mode captures many frames and **aligns and merges them to keep the scene sharp and bright**. It's designed to fight blur, not create it. Point it at a waterfall at dusk and you'll get a bright, fairly sharp image, not silk.

Night mode is excellent for:

- landscapes after sunset and in moonlight
- star fields and the Milky Way on a tripod at a dark site (set the timer and choose Max)
- town lights and illuminated buildings

It switches on automatically in low light. Tap the moon icon to change the duration; longer times only appear when the phone detects it's on a tripod or stable surface. For more on photographing the night sky, see our [Milky Way photography guide](/journal/milky-way-photography.html).

## When you want more: long exposure apps

The built-in effect is fixed at about three seconds. Third-party camera apps let you choose the duration and blur style.

- **Slow Shutter Cam:** a long-standing app with modes for motion blur, light trails and low light, and adjustable durations from fractions of a second to long exposures.
- **Spectre Camera:** from the makers of Halide; uses computational processing to make long exposures handheld, with live preview.
- **Video-blending apps:** several apps take a short video or burst and blend it, giving you control over how much of the clip is used.

All of them still need the phone held very still for the best results.

## iPhone vs a dedicated camera

| | iPhone Live Photo | iPhone app (e.g. Slow Shutter) | Camera + ND filter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Duration | ~3 s fixed | Adjustable | Seconds to minutes |
| Needs a tripod | Ideally | Ideally | Yes |
| Works in bright sun | Yes | Yes | Needs ND filter |
| Image quality | Good, slightly cropped | Good | Best |
| Clouds streaking | No | Sometimes | Yes |

## Quick fixes

**Everything is blurry:** the phone moved. Brace it, use the timer, try again.

**The water is pure white:** too bright. Lower exposure before shooting, or shoot in softer light.

**No Long Exposure option:** it wasn't a Live Photo, or ProRAW was on.

**The blur looks patchy:** three seconds wasn't long enough for the motion. Shoot several and pick the best, or use an app with longer durations.

**Ghostly people or birds:** wait until the frame is clear, or embrace it.

Long exposures are one of the easiest ways to make phone pictures look deliberate. Once you've tried a waterfall, try the sea at dusk, a river under a bridge, or grass moving around a still tree.
