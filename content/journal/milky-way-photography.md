The first time you see the Milky Way properly, at a truly dark site with your eyes adjusted, it's startling. It doesn't look like a faint smudge. It looks like a band of cloud with structure in it, dark lanes of dust and bright knots of stars, stretching from one horizon to the other.

Photographing it is easier than most people think. You don't need a telescope or a tracking mount. A camera that lets you set a long exposure, a wide lens and a sturdy tripod will do. What makes the difference is planning: being in the right place, on the right night, at the right time, with a composition you worked out in daylight.

## What you're photographing

The Milky Way is our galaxy, seen edge-on from inside. The bright, dense part people usually want to photograph is the **galactic core**, in the direction of the constellations Sagittarius and Scorpius. It sits well south of the celestial equator, which affects when and where you can see it.

- **Northern hemisphere:** the core is visible roughly **February to October**. Early in the season it rises before dawn in the south-east; by midsummer it's up around midnight; by autumn it's low in the south-west soon after dark. At higher latitudes it never climbs far above the southern horizon.
- **Southern hemisphere:** the core climbs high overhead in the southern winter (June and July) and is visible for much more of the year.

The rest of the Milky Way, fainter but still beautiful, is visible all year. In the northern summer and autumn, a bright stretch through Cygnus passes almost overhead.

### A note for the UK and northern Europe

At around 51–58° north, the core only reaches a few degrees above the southern horizon, so you need a clear, unlit view to the south (a coastline facing south is ideal). In June and early July, the sky over much of the UK never gets fully dark, because the sun doesn't sink far enough below the horizon. Late July to September, on moonless nights, is the most practical window.

## Step 1: Find a dark sky

Light pollution is the biggest obstacle. A city's glow can wash out the Milky Way from 50 km away.

- Use the [Light Pollution Map](https://www.lightpollutionmap.info/) to find dark areas near you. Aim for Bortle class 1–4 (the darker colours on most maps).
- Look for official [Dark Sky Places](https://darksky.org/what-we-do/international-dark-sky-places/). In the UK these include Galloway Forest Park, Northumberland International Dark Sky Park, Exmoor, Bannau Brycheiniog (Brecon Beacons), Eryri (Snowdonia) and the South Downs.
- Check the direction of the nearest town. A glow on the opposite horizon to the core matters less than one underneath it.

## Step 2: Pick the night

**The moon.** A bright moon washes out the Milky Way as badly as a city. Shoot within about four or five days either side of the new moon, or when the moon has set. [Time and Date](https://www.timeanddate.com/moon/phases/) lists phases and moonrise times for any location.

**Darkness.** Wait until astronomical twilight has ended, roughly 90 minutes or more after sunset (longer in summer at high latitudes).

**Weather.** You need clear skies. Cloud forecasts by height help; high, thin cloud can be invisible to the eye but visible in photos. Low humidity and recent rain often give the crispest skies.

## Step 3: Plan the composition in daylight

The pictures people remember are landscapes with the Milky Way in them, not just star fields. Find your foreground in daylight: a lone tree, a rocky outcrop, a lighthouse, a mountain ridge, a lake for reflections.

Apps such as [PhotoPills](https://www.photopills.com/articles/milky-way-photography-guide) show where the core will be at any time on any date, overlaid on your view through augmented reality. Stand where you'll shoot, check where the core will rise, and frame the foreground accordingly. Note how to get back in the dark.

## Step 4: Gear

**Camera.** Any interchangeable-lens camera with manual control will work. Full-frame sensors handle high ISO more cleanly, but APS-C cameras make excellent Milky Way photos.

**Lens.** A wide angle with a fast aperture. On full frame, 14–24 mm at f/1.4–f/2.8 is ideal. A kit zoom at f/3.5–4 will work, with more noise. Fast primes (20 mm f/1.8, 24 mm f/1.4) are a common and affordable choice.

**Tripod.** Sturdy, and heavy enough not to move in wind. Hang your bag from the centre hook.

**Remote or timer.** A cable release, phone app, or the camera's two-second timer.

**Head torch with a red light.** Red light preserves your night vision, and is considerate to others at the same location.

**Warm clothes and spare batteries.** It is always colder than you expect at 2am, and cold drains batteries.

## Step 5: Camera settings

| Setting | Starting value |
|---|---|
| Mode | Manual (M) |
| Aperture | Widest available (f/1.4–f/2.8) |
| Shutter speed | 10–20 s (see below) |
| ISO | 3200 (range 1600–6400) |
| Focus | Manual, on a bright star (see Step 6) |
| White balance | 3800–4200 K |
| File type | RAW |
| Image stabilisation | Off |
| Long exposure noise reduction | Off (or on for a dedicated dark frame) |

### How long can the shutter stay open?

The Earth rotates, so stars move across the frame. Too long an exposure turns them into short streaks.

The traditional **500 rule** says: divide 500 by your focal length (multiplied by the crop factor) for the maximum exposure in seconds. A 20 mm lens on full frame gives 25 s. On today's high-resolution sensors that's often too long; stars show slight trails when you zoom in.

The **NPF rule** accounts for aperture and pixel size and gives more accurate results. PhotoPills calculates it for your camera. As a practical guide on full frame:

| Focal length | Rough maximum exposure |
|---|---|
| 14 mm | 15–20 s |
| 20 mm | 10–15 s |
| 24 mm | 8–12 s |
| 35 mm | 6–8 s |

Take a test shot, magnify it, and adjust.

## Step 6: Focusing in the dark

Autofocus usually fails at night. Manual focus is reliable if you do it carefully.

1. Switch the lens to manual focus.
2. Turn on live view and point at the brightest star or planet you can see (or a distant light on the horizon).
3. Magnify the live view as far as it goes.
4. Turn the focus ring slowly until the star is as small and sharp a point as possible.
5. Take a test exposure and check by zooming in.
6. Tape the focus ring so it can't move.

Don't trust the infinity mark on the lens; many lenses focus past infinity. A Bahtinov mask makes it easier if you do a lot of night work.

## Step 7: Shoot

1. Compose with the foreground and the Milky Way in place. Use a high-ISO test shot of 3–5 s to check framing quickly.
2. Switch to your full settings and take a frame.
3. Check the histogram: the peak should sit about a third to halfway from the left. If it's against the left edge, increase ISO or exposure time.
4. Look for star trailing and focus at 100% magnification.
5. Take several frames. Stacking multiple identical exposures later reduces noise significantly.

### Foregrounds in the dark

At 15 seconds the foreground will often be a dark silhouette, which can look good. For a detailed foreground, take a separate, much longer exposure at a lower ISO (several minutes) on the same tripod without moving it, ideally during blue hour or by starlight, and blend the two. Avoid lighting the foreground with a bright torch; it rarely looks natural, and it ruins other photographers' shots.

## Step 8: Editing

A raw Milky Way file looks flat and brownish. A light, careful edit brings it to life.

1. **White balance:** cool the image until the sky looks natural, usually 3800–4500 K, with a touch of magenta.
2. **Exposure and contrast:** raise slightly. Add contrast with the tone curve rather than the slider.
3. **Clarity and dehaze:** small amounts bring out structure in the core. Too much makes halos.
4. **Noise reduction:** modern AI denoise tools are very effective. Or stack several frames with free software such as Sequator (Windows) or Starry Landscape Stacker (Mac).
5. **Local adjustments:** brighten the core and darken the edges of the frame with gradients.
6. **Lens corrections:** remove vignetting and distortion.

Keep it believable. Over-saturated purple and orange skies look striking on a phone and dated on a print.

## Common problems

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Stars are short lines | Exposure too long | Shorten shutter or go wider |
| Stars look soft or doughnut-shaped | Focus missed | Refocus with magnified live view |
| Orange glow on horizon | Light pollution | Darker site, or shoot away from towns |
| Image very noisy | Underexposed | Raise ISO, widen aperture, stack frames |
| Blurred image | Tripod moved, wind, or IS on | Weigh down tripod; turn stabilisation off |
| Milky Way faint and grey | Moon up or twilight not over | Check moon phase and astronomical twilight |
