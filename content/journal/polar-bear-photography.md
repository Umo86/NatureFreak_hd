Few animals carry as much weight in a photograph as a polar bear. It's the largest land carnivore on the planet, perfectly adapted to one of the harshest places on Earth, and a symbol of a climate that's changing fast. It's also an animal that very few people will ever see in the wild, which makes the trip a serious investment.

This guide is about getting the most from that investment: choosing where and when to go, what to pack, how to set the camera for white bears on white snow, and how to work safely and ethically.

## Where and when to photograph polar bears

There are two main destinations for photographers, with very different experiences.

### Churchill, Manitoba (Canada)

Churchill, on the western shore of Hudson Bay, is often called the polar bear capital of the world. Every autumn, bears that have spent the summer on land gather along the coast **waiting for the bay to freeze** so they can return to hunting seals on the ice.

- **When:** mid-October to mid-November. Late October and early November are usually the most reliable.
- **How:** from large, high-sided tundra vehicles (operators use names such as Tundra Buggy and Polar Rover) that drive designated tracks in the Churchill Wildlife Management Area. You photograph from open windows and rear outdoor platforms. Some lodges further along the coast run guided walking trips.
- **What you'll see:** bears resting in willows and kelp, walking the shoreline, males sparring, curious bears investigating vehicles. Snow, low sun and short days.
- **Bonus:** the northern lights are often visible in autumn. Churchill in **July and August** offers a different trip, with beluga whales in the river estuary and bears along the coast.

### Svalbard (Norway)

Svalbard is an Arctic archipelago halfway between mainland Norway and the North Pole. Polar bears live on and around its sea ice and coastlines.

- **When:** most expedition cruises run **June to August**, when ships can navigate the fjords and pack ice. Some spring trips (March to May) offer snow and sea ice at their best.
- **How:** from expedition ships and small boats (Zodiacs), scanning the ice edge and coastlines. Sightings depend on ice conditions and luck; good operators spend days searching.
- **What you'll see:** bears on sea ice, walking along shorelines, swimming, hunting, and in summer sometimes feeding on carcasses. Also walrus, Arctic foxes, reindeer and huge seabird colonies.

### The distance rules have changed

Since **1 January 2025**, Svalbard requires visitors to keep at least **300 metres** from polar bears, increasing to **500 metres from 1 March to 30 June**, when bears are especially vulnerable to disturbance. If a bear comes closer, you must move away. These rules, set out by the [Norwegian government](https://www.regjeringen.no/en/whats-new/amendments-to-the-svalbard-environmental-protection-act-provides-better-protection-for-polar-bears-and-seabirds/id3040472/), also restrict drones in protected areas.

For photographers, this changes what's realistic in Svalbard. Frame-filling portraits are much harder; **bears in their landscape** are the natural shot. Plan your lenses and your expectations accordingly.

## Choosing an operator

This trip is only as good as the people running it.

- **Choose photography-focused trips** if photography is your priority. Vehicles and ships carry fewer people, with guaranteed window or deck space.
- **Ask about group size** and how window and platform positions are shared.
- **Ask how they handle bear encounters** and how they follow local rules. Good operators are proud of their ethics.
- **Look for conservation credentials**: support for research, links with organisations like [Polar Bears International](https://polarbearsinternational.org/), and clear guidance for guests.

## Lenses and gear

| Item | Why |
|---|---|
| 100–400 mm or 70–200 mm zoom | Main lens for Churchill vehicles, where bears can be relatively close |
| 500–600 mm, or a 150/200–600 mm zoom | Essential for ships and Svalbard distances |
| 1.4x teleconverter | Extra reach on ships; costs some light |
| Wide-angle (16–35 mm) | Landscapes, auroras, vehicles and people for context |
| Two camera bodies | Avoid changing lenses in snow and wind; one long, one shorter |
| Beanbag or window mount | Tripods are impractical on vehicles and ships |
| 4–6 batteries | Cold drains them fast |
| Fast, large memory cards | Bears do things in bursts |
| Rain/snow covers, microfibre cloths | Spray, snow and condensation |

Image stabilisation helps a lot on a rocking ship; use the mode designed for panning or moving platforms if your lens has one.

## Camera settings for white bears on snow

### Exposure: brighter than the meter thinks

A bear on snow is a large, bright subject in a bright scene. The camera's meter tries to make everything mid-grey, which turns snow and bears dull and grey.

- Add **+1 to +1.7 EV** exposure compensation on snow; less if the bear is against dark water or rocks.
- Turn on **highlight warnings** and check the **histogram**. The peak should sit near, but not touching, the right edge.
- Polar bear fur is creamy or yellowish rather than pure white. If the bear clips to pure white, you've lost the texture that makes it look real.

### A good starting set-up

| Setting | Value |
|---|---|
| Mode | Manual with auto ISO, or aperture priority with a minimum shutter speed |
| Aperture | f/5.6–f/8 (a little stopped down helps depth of field on a whole bear) |
| Shutter speed | 1/1000 s (1/2000 s for running or sparring bears, or from a moving ship) |
| ISO | Auto |
| Focus | Continuous, animal eye detection, burst mode |
| White balance | Daylight or Cloudy, adjusted in RAW |
| File type | RAW |

### White balance

Overcast Arctic light is very blue. Auto white balance can swing between frames. Use a fixed setting (Cloudy works well) and fine-tune in editing, aiming for snow that's neutral and fur that's warm cream.

## Composition

**Get to eye level where you can.** From a tall tundra vehicle you'll be looking down. Shoot from the lowest window, and use moments when the bear lifts its head or stands up to look at you.

**Use the weather.** Blowing snow, low sun and fog make polar bear pictures feel like the Arctic. Don't pack away the camera when the weather turns.

**Show behaviour.** Sparring males in Churchill, a mother with cubs, a bear sniffing the air, rolling in snow, stalking along the ice edge. Behaviour is what makes a portfolio.

**Go wide on purpose.** A small bear in a vast landscape of ice can be more powerful than a close-up, and it's often the only shot the distance rules allow.

**Watch the background.** Vehicles, other ships and buildings intrude. A small change of angle or focal length can remove them.

## Working in extreme cold

Temperatures in Churchill in November can drop well below −20°C with wind chill, and Svalbard is cold even in summer on the water.

- **Batteries:** keep spares in an inside pocket and rotate them. A dead battery often recovers when warmed.
- **Condensation:** when you go from cold to warm, seal the camera in a plastic bag or closed camera bag before you go inside, and let it warm up slowly. Opening it straight away will fog the lens and can let moisture inside.
- **Breath and heat shimmer:** on vehicles and ships, breathing near the lens or shooting through warm air from windows and heaters softens images. Hold your breath, and shoot from open windows or decks.
- **Hands:** thin liner gloves inside mittens let you work buttons. Practise operating the camera with gloves before you go.
- **Screens:** LCDs can slow down in deep cold. Rely on the viewfinder.

## Ethics and safety

Polar bears are dangerous predators, and they are under real pressure. The IUCN lists them as **Vulnerable**, with an estimated global population of around 26,000, and the loss of sea ice as the main threat ([Polar Bears International](https://polarbearsinternational.org/polar-bears/polar-bear-facts/status/)).

- Follow your guides' instructions without exception.
- Never feed, bait or attempt to attract bears.
- Respect local distance rules and don't pressure guides to get closer.
- Never leave vehicles or walk on the tundra in bear country without trained guides.
- Caption honestly. Don't present captive bears as wild.

## Planning checklist

1. Choose destination and month: Churchill (Oct–Nov) or Svalbard (Jun–Aug, or spring).
2. Book early: photography trips often sell out a year or more ahead.
3. Plan lenses for the destination: shorter for Churchill vehicles, longer for Svalbard ships.
4. Test your cold-weather routine and exposure settings on snow before you go.
5. Bring more batteries and storage than you think.
6. Read up on bear behaviour so you can anticipate action.
