There's a particular moment on most safaris when photographers realise their plans were built for the wrong thing. They brought a big lens for distant animals, and a lioness walks past the vehicle close enough to touch. Or they planned for lions, and spent the best light of the morning on a leopard in deep shade. Or they missed the shot entirely because the vehicle was still rocking.

Safari photography rewards preparation more than most kinds of wildlife photography, because you have limited days, limited weight and moments that last seconds. This guide brings that preparation together.

## When and where to go

The right destination depends on what you want to photograph. A few classic options:

- **Masai Mara (Kenya) and Serengeti (Tanzania):** open savanna, big cats and the **Great Migration** of wildebeest and zebra. River crossings in the Mara usually happen between July and October. The southern Serengeti calving season (around January to March) brings predators and newborns.
- **Okavango Delta and Chobe (Botswana):** water, elephants, wild dogs, birds, and boat-based photography. The dry season (roughly June to October) concentrates animals.
- **Kruger and private reserves (South Africa):** excellent access and infrastructure. Private reserves bordering Kruger allow off-road driving for leopards and close sightings.
- **Etosha (Namibia):** waterholes in a pale desert landscape, superb in the dry season, with floodlit waterholes at night.
- **South Luangwa (Zambia):** walking safaris and leopards.

**Dry season vs green season.** The dry season (varies by region but often July to October in southern and East Africa) makes animals easier to find because they gather around water, and vegetation is thinner. The green season brings dramatic skies, lush backdrops, baby animals and fewer vehicles, at the cost of some rain and longer grass.

## Choose the right kind of safari

For photography, how you travel matters as much as where.

- **Private or photographic vehicles:** if your budget allows one thing, make it this. You control how long you stay at a sighting and where the vehicle is positioned for the light.
- **Small groups:** if sharing, ask how many guests per vehicle. A guaranteed outside seat or a row each makes a big difference.
- **Open vs closed vehicles:** open-sided vehicles (common in southern Africa) give freer angles; pop-top vehicles (common in East Africa) let you shoot standing through the roof.
- **Guides who understand photography:** a guide who knows to position the vehicle with the light behind you and turn off the engine is worth more than any lens.

## Lenses and gear

### The core kit

| Item | Why |
|---|---|
| 100–400 mm, 150–600 mm or 200–600 mm zoom | Main wildlife lens: framing flexibility is vital from a vehicle |
| 24–70 mm or 24–105 mm | Close animals, landscapes, camp and people |
| Two camera bodies | No lens changes in dust; instant switch between wide and tight |
| Beanbag | Rests on the door or roof; steadier than handholding, quicker than a tripod |
| 4+ batteries and charging cables | Charging may be limited to certain hours at camp |
| Large, fast memory cards and a backup | You'll shoot more than you think |
| Blower, cloths, sensor-safe cleaning kit | Dust gets everywhere |
| Lens covers or a pillowcase | Protection from dust on bumpy tracks |

A teleconverter is handy for birds and shy animals in good light. A fast prime (500 mm f/4 or 600 mm f/4) is wonderful if you own one, but a modern zoom is more versatile from a vehicle.

### Watch the weight limits

Light aircraft that connect camps in Kenya, Tanzania, Botswana and Zambia often limit luggage to around **15 kg per person in soft bags**, including hand luggage. Check with your operator. Some offer an extra seat for camera gear; some guides keep beanbags at camp, so you can travel with them empty and fill them with rice or beans locally.

## Camera settings for safari

The light changes constantly and animals go from still to sprinting without warning. A flexible set-up saves shots.

| Setting | Value |
|---|---|
| Mode | Manual with auto ISO |
| Aperture | Wide open (f/5.6–f/6.3), or f/8 for groups of animals |
| Shutter speed | 1/1000 s general; 1/2000–1/3200 s for running animals and birds |
| ISO | Auto, max 6400–12,800 |
| Focus | Continuous, animal-eye detection, zone for groups |
| Drive | Burst |
| Image stabilisation | On (sport mode if available) |
| File type | RAW |

**Save a second custom mode** for low-light portraits (1/250 s, wide open) and a third for slow-shutter panning (1/30 s, f/8), so you can switch in a second.

**Exposure compensation** matters: dark animals (buffalo, wildebeest) against bright skies or pale grass need +0.7 to +1 EV; pale animals or bright birds against dark backgrounds need −0.7.

## Shooting from a vehicle

- **Ask the driver to switch off the engine** before you shoot. Vibration through the chassis softens images at long focal lengths.
- **Shoot from a beanbag** on the door or roof, with the lens resting on it and your hand on top.
- **Ask for positioning.** Say early on that you'd like the sun behind the vehicle and to be at the animal's eye level where possible. Good guides will anticipate where an animal is heading and get there first.
- **Get low.** In open vehicles, a lower seat or shooting over the door gives a more intimate angle than shooting from the roof.
- **Keep still and quiet.** Animals that are relaxed with a vehicle can be disturbed by people standing up or talking loudly.

## Light and timing

Morning and evening game drives aren't just when animals are most active; they're when the light is best.

- **First light to about 9am** and **from about 4pm to sunset** give warm, directional light and long shadows.
- **Midday** is harsh, hot and quiet. Use it for rest, editing, birds at camp, or close details in shade. Black-and-white conversions often work well with midday light.
- **Backlight** at sunrise and sunset creates rim-lit fur and dust; expose for the highlights and let the animal fall into silhouette.
- **Storms** in the green season give dark skies behind sunlit animals. Stay out for them if you can.

## Composition beyond the portrait

On a first safari it's tempting to fill the frame with every animal. After the first day, start mixing it up.

- **Eyes and eye level.** Sharp eyes, ideally with a catchlight, and the lowest angle you can get.
- **Behaviour.** Interaction between animals, hunting, playing, grooming, mothers and young.
- **Habitat.** Wide shots of elephants under storm clouds, giraffes against a red sky, a lion on a kopje. These often become the favourite pictures.
- **Details.** Elephant skin, zebra stripes, a cheetah's tear marks, the feathers of a lilac-breasted roller.
- **Motion.** A panned zebra herd at 1/30 s or a dust-filled wildebeest crossing.
- **Space to move.** Give running or walking animals room in front of them.

## Dust, heat and protecting gear

- Change lenses inside the vehicle with your back to the wind, or better, don't change at all by using two bodies.
- Cover gear with a cloth or pillowcase on dusty tracks.
- Use a blower first and a cloth second; wiping dust can scratch.
- Keep cameras out of direct sun during midday breaks.
- Back up images every evening to a laptop, tablet or portable drive.

## Safari ethics

The best safari photographers are also the most patient and respectful guests.

- **Don't ask guides to break rules.** Leaving tracks where off-road driving isn't allowed, crowding a sighting or getting too close to a cheetah on a hunt can cost animals their meal or their cubs.
- **Respect sighting limits.** Many reserves limit vehicles at a sighting and how long they can stay.
- **No calling, whistling or tapping** to make an animal look up.
- **No drones** without explicit permits; most parks ban them.
- **Be careful with locations.** Rhino and other targeted species are vulnerable to poaching; don't geotag sensitive sightings.
- **Caption honestly** and never present captive or semi-captive animals as wild.

## Packing checklist

1. Two camera bodies and two zooms (telephoto and standard)
2. Beanbag (empty, fill locally)
3. Batteries, chargers, a multi-socket adapter, a power bank
4. Memory cards and a backup device
5. Cleaning kit: blower, cloths, sensor swabs
6. Lens and camera covers for dust
7. Binoculars (8x42 or 10x42)
8. Neutral-coloured clothing (avoid bright colours, and avoid military-style camouflage, which some African countries restrict for civilians)
9. Hat, sunglasses, sun cream and a warm layer for cold early mornings
10. A soft bag that meets the bush-flight weight limit
